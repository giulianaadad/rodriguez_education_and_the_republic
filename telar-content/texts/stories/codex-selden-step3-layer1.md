The tribute pages reveal how much of the Indigenous writing tradition remained intact after the conquest. Clothing, shields, feathers, agricultural products, and other goods are represented through carefully drawn images rather than written Spanish lists. Quantities are also communicated through visual signs. These were not simply decorations added to a European document. They belonged to a long Indigenous tradition of using visual writing to organize economic and political information. The subject itself also demonstrates continuity. Before the Spanish conquest, tribute records helped rulers keep track of resources flowing from conquered provinces toward the imperial center. Writing was therefore closely connected to power because it allowed a state to classify territories, measure obligations, and record wealth. Even though the Codex Mendoza was produced under Spanish colonial rule, Indigenous visual conventions continued to preserve knowledge about the earlier Mexica imperial system. The political authority surrounding the writing had changed, but many of the techniques used to organize and communicate information remained recognizable.
