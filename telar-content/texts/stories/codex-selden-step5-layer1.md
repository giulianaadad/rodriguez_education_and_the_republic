title: "Learn more"
---

The Codex Mendoza does not show a simple story in which Spanish alphabetic writing arrived and Indigenous writing disappeared. Instead, it records a period in which two writing systems occupied the same pages and depended upon one another. Indigenous painter-scribes preserved pictorial conventions for representing places, people, tribute, numbers, history, and social life. Those forms represent continuity. Yet important changes occurred around them. European paper and manuscript practices entered the production process, Spanish alphabetic annotations were added, and Indigenous knowledge was reorganized for readers connected to Spanish colonial government. Most importantly, the audience and political purpose of the writing changed. The Codex Mendoza therefore demonstrates that encounters between writing systems involve negotiation rather than simple replacement. Signs can survive while their meanings, audiences, and functions shift. Writing systems are not only technologies for recording language. They are cultural systems whose meanings depend upon communities, institutions, readers, and power. When those conditions change, writing changes with them.
