---
title: "Learn more"
---

The Codex Mendoza becomes especially revealing when Spanish alphabetic writing appears directly beside Indigenous pictorial writing. The two systems occupy the same physical page, but they communicate information differently. Indigenous painter-scribes continued to represent places, people, quantities, tribute, and events visually, while Spanish writers added alphabetic explanations around those images. Instead of one system immediately replacing the other, the page became a space where the two systems coexisted. This coexistence was necessary partly because the intended European audience did not possess the cultural knowledge needed to interpret the Indigenous images independently. The Spanish annotations therefore acted as a bridge between the pictorial signs and the new reader. At the same time, this translation changed the object. Information that once circulated within an Indigenous system of knowledge was now being explained through Spanish vocabulary and categories. The physical survival of the Indigenous images therefore represents continuity, while the addition of alphabetic explanations represents a major transformation in how those images could be read.
