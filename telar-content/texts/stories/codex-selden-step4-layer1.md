---
title: "Learn more"
---

The later portions of the Codex Mendoza demonstrate that pictorial writing could record much more than political events or taxes. Images portray childhood, education, labor, punishment, clothing, gender roles, and everyday activities. For example, scenes showing mothers teaching daughters to spin and weave communicate relationships between age, work, family, and education visually. This reveals another important continuity. Indigenous writers continued to use images as meaningful units capable of explaining how society was organized. A single scene can communicate who performs an activity, what tools are involved, and how knowledge passes from one generation to another. Spanish annotations can clarify those scenes for an unfamiliar reader, but the images themselves still carry much of the information. The Codex therefore challenges the assumption that alphabetic writing automatically provides a more complete form of communication. Indigenous pictorial writing preserved kinds of social information especially well because gesture, clothing, objects, and relationships could all appear simultaneously. What changed was increasingly the framework through which Europeans interpreted that information.
